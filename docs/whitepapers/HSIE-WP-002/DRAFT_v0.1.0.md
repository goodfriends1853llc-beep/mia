# Reality ≠ Representation

## A Systems Architecture for Keeping AI Records, Inferences, and State Separate from the World They Describe

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence systems do not operate directly on reality. They operate on representations of reality: text, records, measurements, images, sensor outputs, database fields, retrieved documents, embeddings, summaries, labels, profiles, model outputs, and previously stored state.

That distinction is easy to state and easy to violate.

When a system collapses a representation into the thing it represents, stale records can become current truth, model inference can become accepted fact, generated summaries can become authoritative memory, reported intention can become assumed consent, and an execution log can become false proof that an external effect actually occurred.

This paper develops **Reality ≠ Representation** as a systems-architecture discipline for consequential AI. The principle requires AI environments to preserve the difference between the world, evidence about the world, records derived from that evidence, interpretations generated from records, governed state accepted by authorized actors, and later outcomes that may confirm, contradict, or supersede earlier representations.

The paper argues that trustworthy AI systems require explicit provenance, time, identity, authority, scope, uncertainty, evidence, supersession, and promotion rules around representations. It further argues that human beings must not be reduced to stored profiles, and that current state must not silently overwrite historical state.

The result is not a claim that software can obtain perfect access to reality.

It is a narrower architectural requirement:

> **A system should know when it is holding a representation, preserve where that representation came from, and refuse to promote it beyond the evidence that supports it.**

---

# 1. The Problem

Every digital system works through representation.

A database stores fields rather than the physical world.

A camera stores an image rather than the scene itself.

A sensor stores a measurement rather than the phenomenon measured.

A transcript stores words rather than the full human interaction.

A model stores parameters rather than the lived reality represented in its training data.

An AI assistant stores context rather than the person.

These distinctions are not merely philosophical.

They become operationally important when representations are used to make decisions, allocate authority, trigger actions, update memory, or generate future AI context.

A representation may be incomplete.

It may be stale.

It may be wrong.

It may be accurate only within a narrow scope.

It may have been produced by an untrusted source.

It may describe a past condition that no longer exists.

It may be an inference rather than an observation.

It may have been generated by another AI system.

It may have been superseded by later evidence.

It may be a faithful description and still not carry authority.

The architecture must therefore preserve a distinction between **what exists**, **what was observed**, **what was recorded**, **what was inferred**, **what was accepted**, and **what was later verified**.

---

# 2. The Core Principle

The governing statement is:

> **REALITY ≠ REPRESENTATION**

This does not mean representations are useless.

Representations are necessary.

Without them, software cannot reason about the world at all.

The requirement is that the system does not confuse its representation with the thing represented.

A person's profile is not the person.

A preference record is not a permanent preference.

A medical note is not the entire human condition.

A financial record is not identical to economic reality.

A governance policy is not enforcement.

A model confidence score is not truth.

A returned API response is not necessarily verified external effect.

A generated answer is not automatically accepted state.

This distinction should remain visible throughout the system lifecycle.

---

# 3. Representation Layers

A consequential AI environment may contain several layers that should remain separable.

A useful conceptual sequence is:

**Reality → Evidence → Record → Interpretation → Governed State → AI Context → Action → Outcome → Calibration**

**Reality** is the underlying person, event, object, condition, or external state that exists independently of the software's description.

**Evidence** is material that supports a statement about reality: direct observation, a document, a measurement, a sensor reading, testimony, a transaction record, or another trace.

**Record** is a stored representation derived from evidence or assertion.

**Interpretation** is a conclusion, classification, summary, prediction, inference, or model-generated explanation based on one or more records.

**Governed State** is information accepted into an authoritative system state under defined rules and authority.

**AI Context** is the subset of governed or candidate information supplied to an AI capability for a particular task.

**Action** is what an authorized human or machine does.

**Outcome** is what subsequently occurs.

**Calibration** is the process of comparing prior representations, interpretations, or expectations against later evidence and outcomes.

These layers may interact, but they should not silently collapse into one another.

---

# 4. Evidence Does Not Eliminate Interpretation

Evidence is stronger than unsupported assertion, but evidence still requires interpretation.

A photograph may show an object while leaving its context unclear.

A sensor reading may be accurate while the sensor is poorly positioned for the question being asked.

A transaction may prove that money moved while not proving why it moved.

A message may prove that words were sent while not proving motive.

A record may prove what a system stored while not proving the stored value was correct.

For this reason, evidence should carry provenance and scope.

The system should preserve at least:

- what the evidence object is;
- who or what produced it;
- when it was produced;
- what entity or event it is associated with;
- what transformation produced the current representation;
- what uncertainty remains;
- what authority, if any, the evidence supports;
- whether later evidence supersedes or contradicts it.

W3C PROV provides an established technical precedent for modeling provenance through entities, activities, agents, derivation, attribution, and time. HSIE does not claim conformance with W3C PROV, and TB-SUB-001 retains its own Entity/Event/Relationship foundation, but the broader provenance problem is shared: information becomes more assessable when its origin and transformation history remain reconstructable.

---

# 5. Inference Is Not Accepted State

Modern AI systems are powerful inference engines.

They can infer preferences, intentions, categories, risks, likely outcomes, emotional tone, similarity, priority, and relationships from incomplete information.

That capability is useful.

It is also dangerous when inference is silently promoted into authoritative state.

Consider the difference between:

**“The model inferred that the user prefers X.”**

and:

**“The user prefers X.”**

The second sentence has stronger epistemic force.

It should not be created merely because the first sentence exists.

The same applies to summaries.

An AI-generated summary may be useful as a working representation of a longer record. It does not automatically replace the source material, and it should not erase disagreements, uncertainty, or omitted details.

A governed system therefore needs explicit promotion rules.

Inference may remain candidate context.

Human acceptance may promote some inferences.

Independent evidence may strengthen others.

Later evidence may supersede earlier conclusions.

But promotion should be an Event, not an invisible side effect of model generation.

---

# 6. Human Reality Must Not Collapse Into a Profile

The Reality ≠ Representation principle becomes especially important when systems model people.

A person is not reducible to:

a profile;

a memory summary;

a risk score;

a preference vector;

a diagnosis;

a recommendation history;

a behavioral label;

a set of embeddings;

or a longitudinal record.

These representations may support useful assistance.

They do not become the human being.

Human reality remains upstream.

A healthy architecture should therefore preserve a chain such as:

**Human Reality → Evidence → Human Record → Governed Human Model → AI Context → Assistance → Action → Outcome → Calibration**

The human remains distinct from every downstream representation.

This prevents a common systems failure: old data becomes a permanent sentence.

A person can change.

A preference can change.

Authority can change.

Consent can change.

Relationships can change.

Context can change.

The architecture must allow current state to update without rewriting historical state.

---

# 7. Time Is Part of Meaning

A representation without time can become misleading even when the representation was once correct.

“Authorized” may be true at one moment and false later.

“Lives at this address” may be true during one period and false during another.

“Prefers this workflow” may be current, historical, or unknown.

“Credential valid” may depend on expiration and revocation.

“Consent granted” may be scoped to a task and period.

Time is therefore not merely metadata.

It can change the meaning and validity of a representation.

The architecture should distinguish:

**valid then** from **valid now**;

**known then** from **known now**;

and **recorded then** from **verified now**.

A later correction should supersede an earlier representation when appropriate, but it should not erase the fact that the earlier representation existed and influenced prior decisions.

---

# 8. Representation Does Not Create Authority

A record can describe authority without creating authority.

A database field may say `AUTHORIZED = TRUE`.

A model may predict that a person would probably approve.

A historical record may show that similar actions were approved before.

None of those facts necessarily creates present authority.

Authority must come from the governing source defined by the system.

The same rule applies to consent.

Past consent is not automatically current consent.

Consent for one purpose is not automatically consent for another purpose.

A stored representation of consent should therefore preserve the subject, scope, purpose, time, authority basis, and revocation state that give it meaning.

This is where Reality ≠ Representation directly connects to MIA's bounded-authority model.

---

# 9. Execution Records Are Not External Reality

The distinction also applies to machine action.

An execution record may establish that software attempted an operation.

It may establish that a tool returned a result.

It may establish that a provider accepted a request.

Those facts do not necessarily establish that the intended external effect occurred.

For consequential systems:

> **EXECUTION ≠ EFFECT**

and:

> **RETURNED OUTPUT ≠ VERIFIED EXTERNAL CONSEQUENCE**

This is a special case of Reality ≠ Representation.

The execution trace is a representation of what the runtime observed.

The external world must still be verified according to the effect contract required by the operation.

When verification is unavailable, uncertainty should remain explicit.

`UNKNOWN` is preferable to invented certainty.

---

# 10. Memory Requires Provenance

AI memory creates a second-order representation problem.

A system may remember not only facts but also prior summaries, inferences, corrections, and model-generated descriptions.

If memory is allowed to flatten these categories, it can convert interpretation into apparent fact over time.

A robust memory system should therefore retain provenance.

It should be possible to distinguish:

a statement directly supplied by the subject;

a third-party report;

an imported record;

a model inference;

a human-accepted conclusion;

a correction;

a superseded representation;

and an unresolved contradiction.

Memory should preserve continuity without pretending continuity means certainty.

Reconstructability is more valuable than a clean but misleading single story.

---

# 11. Architectural Requirements

Reality ≠ Representation produces a practical systems discipline.

A conforming architecture should be able to preserve, at minimum, the identity of the represented entity, the identity of the representation itself, provenance, source type, time, scope, authority, uncertainty, transformation history, evidence relationships, supersession history, and whether the representation is observed, reported, inferred, accepted, verified, disputed, or unresolved.

Those labels are not proposed here as new TB-SUB foundational primitives.

They are candidate representational states that implementations may encode according to their own governed contracts.

The deeper requirement is structural:

**the system must not silently erase the difference between the world, evidence about the world, and conclusions drawn from that evidence.**

# 12. Relationship to TB-SUB, MIA, and HSIE

TB-SUB-001 v1.0.0 remains the foundational substrate.

Its primitives remain:

**Entity**

**Event**

**Relationship**

Reality ≠ Representation does not amend that foundation.

Instead, it governs how representations built from those structures should be interpreted and promoted.

MIA operationalizes parts of this discipline through identity, evidence, authority, effect verification, commit, receipts, replay, and preserved failure state.

HSIE applies the same discipline across a broader governed-intelligence environment.

The principle is therefore cross-cutting.

It is not owned by one model, provider, application, or runtime.

---

# 13. Relationship to Existing Technical Guidance

The principle has adjacent precedent in established technical work.

The W3C PROV family treats provenance as information about entities, activities, and responsible agents involved in producing data or things. Its purpose includes enabling assessments of quality, reliability, and trustworthiness.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 emphasizes continuous governance, mapping, measurement, management, documentation, and lifecycle risk management for AI systems.

NIST AI 600-1, the Generative AI Profile, includes content-provenance guidance and describes provenance tracking as a way to record the origin and history of digital content and inputs.

These frameworks do not establish the Reality ≠ Representation doctrine described here, and this paper does not claim conformance with them.

They demonstrate that provenance, context, documentation, responsibility, and lifecycle traceability are established technical concerns rather than decorative metadata.

---

# 14. Failure Modes

Systems violate Reality ≠ Representation when they:

treat inference as fact;

treat a stored profile as the person;

treat a policy statement as enforcement;

treat authentication as authority;

treat old consent as current consent;

treat a tool return as verified effect;

treat a summary as equivalent to its source;

treat current state as if it had always been true;

erase contradictory history;

or treat missing evidence as evidence of absence.

These failures share the same structural error:

**the representation is promoted beyond what its evidence, authority, or scope supports.**

---

# 15. Claim Boundary

This paper is an architectural and epistemic systems paper.

It does not claim that MIA, HSIE, or any AI system can obtain direct and complete access to objective reality.

It does not claim that provenance eliminates uncertainty.

It does not claim that human acceptance makes a statement objectively true.

It does not claim that every representation can be externally verified.

It does not claim independent validation of every architecture discussed.

The narrower claim is:

> **Consequential AI systems can be designed to preserve the difference between reality and representation, track how representations were produced, govern how they are promoted, and retain uncertainty when stronger evidence is unavailable.**

---

# 16. Conclusion

Artificial intelligence will increasingly operate through records about people, organizations, systems, and the physical world.

The danger is not merely that a model may generate an incorrect answer.

The deeper danger is that systems may forget what kind of thing an answer is.

A prediction can become a profile.

A profile can become policy.

A policy can become automated action.

A log can become proof.

A historical record can become present identity.

An inference can become “truth” simply because it survived long enough in memory.

Reality ≠ Representation is a refusal to allow those promotions to happen invisibly.

The system may model reality.

It may store evidence.

It may infer.

It may summarize.

It may remember.

It may act.

But it must preserve the boundary between the world and what the system believes, records, or says about the world.

That boundary is not a philosophical luxury.

It is part of the architecture of trustworthy consequential AI.
